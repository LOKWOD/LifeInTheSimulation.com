FIELD GUIDE 008

Build a Personal Information Diet

Updated August 17, 2026 · Practical field guide

Information quality is not solved by consuming more. A useful diet gives urgent facts a fast path, important analysis a slow path and everything else a reason to earn attention.

Define the decisions information supports

List the domains where current knowledge changes action: local weather, work, finance, health, family logistics, civic participation or a technical field.

Separate decision-relevant information from ambient awareness. Many updates create emotional motion without changing anything the reader can responsibly do.

Create source tiers

Tier one is primary material: documents, data, direct statements and original research. Tier two is trusted analysis. Tier three is discovery—feeds and commentary that point toward sources but do not finish the work.

Know when the stakes require moving down the chain toward original evidence rather than repeating a summary.

Schedule intake by urgency

Weather warnings and direct work messages may require alerts. General news and commentary can be reviewed at one or two scheduled times.

Turn off notifications for information that remains equally useful an hour later.

Use a stopping rule

Choose a time limit or a defined source set. When the session ends, write the few facts that changed your understanding and the questions that remain.

Without a stopping rule, every answer becomes another link and the session loses a usable conclusion.

Review the emotional residue

Notice which sources repeatedly produce outrage, helplessness or certainty without improving accuracy. Emotional intensity is not proof of importance.

Remove sources that mislead, duplicate or consume attention beyond their decision value. Preserve room for long-form work that changes models rather than merely updating events.

Field checklist

  • Decision-relevant domains listed
  • Primary sources identified
  • Trusted analysis limited
  • Discovery treated as leads
  • Urgent alerts separated
  • News windows scheduled
  • Stopping rule set
  • Key facts summarized
  • Emotional residue reviewed
  • Low-value sources removed

Frequently asked questions

How often should news be checked?

Responsibilities differ, but many people can handle general news in one or two sessions while preserving true alerts.

Are newsletters better?

They are bounded, but quality and volume still need review.

What is a primary source?

Original data, documents, recordings, research or statements closest to the claim.

How can bias be reduced?

Compare credible sources and inspect original evidence when stakes are high.
