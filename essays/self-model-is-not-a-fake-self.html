TRANSMISSION 034CONSCIOUSNESS

A Self-Model Is Not a Fake Self

The mind may continuously model the body, perspective, agency and biography. That would make the self constructed in an ordinary biological sense—not counterfeit, disposable or explained away.

8 min read1,347 wordsPublished September 7, 2026 Status: evidence openBy Life in the Simulation Editorial Desk

IN THIS TRANSMISSION

The phrase self-model can sound like an eviction notice. If the brain constructs a model of the body, its point of view and its history, perhaps the self is only a simulation: a useful fiction watching another fiction. That conclusion moves faster than the evidence.

A model is an organized representation used to coordinate prediction and action. A nervous system must keep track of which body it controls, where that body is, what it needs, what it has done and what other people are likely to expect from it. Calling that organization a model says something about how the work may be done. It does not establish that the organism, experience or responsibility is unreal.

Begin with the smallest defensible claim

Human self-experience has several distinguishable parts. There is ownership of a body, location in a perspective, authorship of some actions, access to thoughts and feelings, continuity across memory, and a social identity that persists in records and relationships. These components usually arrive together, so ordinary language compresses them into one word: me.

Research can pull some components apart. Body-ownership experiments can alter how strongly a participant reports that a visible artificial hand belongs to them when visual and tactile information are coordinated. Studies also investigate how proprioceptive uncertainty changes that effect. These results show that body ownership is responsive to multisensory evidence. They do not show that people have no bodies, that every part of identity is equally malleable, or that consciousness has been explained.

The self begins inside a regulated body

Interoception is the sensing, interpretation and integration of signals from within the body. Contemporary research connects it to regulation, affect, memory and aspects of self-experience. Hunger, breathlessness, heartbeat, pain and temperature are not philosophical decorations. They help determine which conditions matter to the organism and which actions become urgent.

Even this apparently simple domain requires care. Researchers distinguish objective task performance, subjective confidence, attention to bodily signals and the concepts people use to report them. A person can feel highly attuned without performing accurately on a laboratory task, and a task may capture only a narrow bodily channel. The word interoception names a research area, not a single meter for inner truth.

One influential family of theories treats perception and bodily regulation as forms of inference: the nervous system uses prior expectations and incoming signals to estimate the causes and needs of its body. This is a theoretical framework with productive models and open questions. It is not a settled demonstration that a hidden prediction engine manufactures all experience.

A useful model can be transparent to its user

You do not normally experience a data structure labeled current body ownership. You experience this hand as your hand and this view as here. Some self-model theories describe this immediacy as transparency: the representational process is not itself presented as an object, so its content feels simply given.

That idea can explain why experimental perturbations are surprising. If ownership were a consciously solved equation, a participant could watch the variables change. Instead, the result arrives as a changed feeling of mine-ness. But transparency is not proof of deception. Vision is also largely transparent: you see a cup, not the processing that estimated its edges. The cup does not become fictional because perception required construction.

Construction is not fabrication

A bridge is constructed and real. A legal promise is constructed and can still bind people. A memory is reconstructed and may still be accurate enough to identify what happened. The contrast that matters is not constructed versus real. It is better constructed versus worse constructed, more or less constrained, and fit or unfit for a particular purpose.

A self-representation is constrained by a living body, a physical location, action consequences, other people and records that resist revision. It can also be wrong. Illness can distort bodily signals; memory can omit or blend events; social feedback can be manipulative; a person can misattribute the source of an action. Error is possible precisely because the model is answerable to something beyond its current content.

This is an inference from the research rather than a direct experimental result: the most useful conception of self may be neither an indivisible inner object nor a disposable fiction. It may be a maintained organization that lets an organism integrate needs, action, memory and social commitments across time.

Do not collapse three levels of argument

  1. Empirical: particular aspects of self-experience vary with sensory integration, bodily state, memory and social context.
  2. Computational: models can describe how a system estimates body, agency or perspective from uncertain signals.
  3. Metaphysical: claims about whether a self ultimately exists, is fundamental or is an illusion.

Evidence at the first level can support or constrain the second. It rarely settles the third by itself. A body-ownership result does not decide whether persons are metaphysically fundamental. A philosophical argument that no simple, permanent ego exists does not erase the biological organism or the experience under discussion.

A process can still be responsible

Some people worry that a constructed self cannot be a moral agent. But responsibility never required a miniature unchanging observer inside the skull. It requires capacities distributed across time: understanding a reason, anticipating consequences, controlling some actions, remembering commitments, responding to evidence and repairing harm.

Those capacities can be impaired, supported and developed. That is compatible with a process view. A company remains accountable even though its staff and documents change; a living body remains the same organism through cellular turnover; a promise persists because practices maintain it. Continuity can be organized rather than made from an immutable substance.

Do not transfer the concept to AI by resemblance

An AI system can generate first-person sentences and maintain a compact representation of its role, tools or conversation state. That is evidence of useful self-reference inside a task. It is not enough to establish embodied selfhood, felt ownership, consciousness or moral patienthood.

Conversely, saying that humans use self-models does not imply that any system with a state description has a human-like self. Similar vocabulary can conceal different mechanisms and consequences. The responsible comparison names the capacity—error monitoring, autobiographical memory, bodily regulation, social perspective—then asks what evidence supports it in each system.

A practical self-model audit

The theory becomes useful when it makes error correctable without turning identity into a performance dashboard. Take one important judgment about yourself and write five lines:

  1. Claim: What exactly do I believe about myself?
  2. Inputs: Which bodily signals, memories, outcomes and other people contributed?
  3. Constraints: What evidence would resist the story if it were convenient but wrong?
  4. Purpose: Which action is this self-description helping me choose?
  5. Revision: What new observation would make the description more accurate?

Do not use the exercise to dissolve every commitment into ambiguity. Use it to separate a person from one provisional account of that person. “I failed at this attempt” is an event. “I am incapable” is a broad model update that needs much more evidence.

What remains open

There is no consensus theory that explains the full unity, continuity and phenomenal character of self-consciousness. Researchers disagree about how much predictive processing explains, how to measure interoception, and whether representational accounts capture the felt first-person character of experience. Philosophers also disagree about whether selfhood is best understood as an entity, process, perspective, narrative or family of capacities.

The honest conclusion is limited but meaningful. Self-experience is structured, embodied and revisable. Some of its components can be experimentally altered. Models help explain that structure. None of this licenses the leap from constructed to fake.
