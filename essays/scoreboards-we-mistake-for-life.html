The Scoreboards We Mistake for Life

6 min read

Humans love scoreboards because scoreboards compress complexity. Revenue summarizes commercial activity. A grade summarizes academic performance. A follower count summarizes an audience. A health score summarizes a collection of measurements.

Compression is useful. It is also lossy.

When the metric becomes the target

Once a measure carries rewards, people naturally optimize for it. That can produce better outcomes when the metric closely tracks what matters. It can also produce theater: activity that improves the score while degrading the underlying reality.

A business can hit a quarterly target while hollowing out trust. A person can improve a social-media metric while feeling more isolated. A student can optimize for the test while understanding less.

A scoreboard is a representation of a game. It is not the game.

Keep multiple instruments

The answer is not to reject measurement. It is to avoid allowing one number to monopolize judgment. Quantitative indicators work best beside qualitative reality: conversations, observation, long-term outcomes and direct experience.

Ask a simple question whenever a number becomes emotionally powerful: what is this actually measuring, what is it failing to measure, and what behavior does it reward?

Return to the thing

Money is useful because it coordinates exchange. Productivity metrics can reveal bottlenecks. Fitness data can expose patterns. The problem begins when a proxy quietly becomes an identity.

You are allowed to use the scoreboard without living for it.
