AI and the End of Knowing Who Made What

7 min read

For most of the internet’s history, seeing was imperfect evidence. It was still evidence. Generative systems weaken that shortcut. Images, voices, video and writing can now be produced at a quality and scale that make casual inspection unreliable.

The scarce resource changes

When content becomes cheap, provenance becomes valuable. The question shifts from “Does this look real?” to “Where did this come from, who is accountable for it, and can the chain be verified?”

This is not a reason to distrust everything. Total skepticism is as useless as total credulity. It is a reason to become more disciplined about consequential claims.

In a synthetic-media world, confidence should follow provenance—not vibes.

A new everyday literacy

Before acting on surprising media, find the original source. Look for independent reporting. Check whether the account publishing it has an identity and history. Separate a screenshot from the document it claims to depict. Be cautious when urgency is part of the pitch.

Most low-stakes content does not require forensic analysis. High-stakes claims do.

The human layer

AI also blurs authorship in less dramatic ways. A report may be drafted by a model and edited by a person. A photograph may be real but retouched. A voice may be translated while retaining the speaker’s tone. “Human-made” and “AI-made” become less useful as binary categories.

Accountability remains the cleaner question: who stands behind this, and what process produced it?
